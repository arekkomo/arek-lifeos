# Script to Scene Analysis

Analyse the scene before deciding shots: a shot list records a plan; it does not discover what the scene is about. The core pass below synthesises the scene-analysis and actor-direction approaches in [[Directing Craft Reference Sources#1 — Rabiger|[1]]] and [[Directing Craft Reference Sources#2 — Weston|[2]]].

## Director’s scene card

| Field | Question to answer | Production consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Dramatic event** | What is different when the scene ends? | Defines the scene’s required change, not merely its topic. |
| **POV / audience alignment** | Whose discovery, pressure or loss should the viewer principally experience? | Determines whose reaction and information take priority. |
| **Objective** | What does each principal character actively try to get now? | Gives actors playable action rather than a result such as “be angry.” |
| **Obstacle / conflict** | Who or what makes that objective difficult? | Identifies the pressure that needs to be visible, audible or felt. |
| **Tactics and beats** | When does a character change tactic because the prior one fails? | Marks units for performance shifts and possible editorial changes. |
| **Turn** | At what moment do expectation, power, information or commitment reverse? | Candidate for a change in staging, framing, sound or cut rhythm. |
| **Emotional / power arc** | How do each character’s vulnerability, control or distance change from start to finish? | Supplies the visual direction; it does not mechanically dictate a lens or shot size. |
| **Given circumstances** | What is physically true: location, time, props, entrances, exits, prior action? | Establishes geography, continuity and practical staging constraints. |

### Beat notation

Write each beat as **trigger → tactic → response → new condition**. This keeps a beat observable and prevents confusing a line break with a dramatic change. For example: “The message is revealed → Mara deflects with humour → Jo refuses to laugh → Mara loses control of the room.” The objective/tactic framing is grounded in [[Directing Craft Reference Sources#2 — Weston|[2]]].

## Staging pass before coverage

Map where bodies, sightlines, props and exits must be at the start, through each beat, and at the turn. Blocking should expose or withhold relationships and information rather than simply keep performers moving. Identify: (1) the clean master geography, (2) moments that require a reaction, (3) contact or prop continuity, (4) eyeline dependencies, and (5) the physical action that can motivate a reframing or move. [[Directing Craft Reference Sources#1 — Rabiger|[1]]] [[Directing Craft Reference Sources#3 — Katz|[3]]]

## Deliverable: one-page scene brief

```text
SCENE / DRAFT / PURPOSE:
Dramatic event:
POV / audience alignment:
Character A — objective / obstacle / tactics:
Character B — objective / obstacle / tactics:
Beat map (trigger → tactic → response → new condition):
Turn and its evidence on the page:
Power/emotional arc (start → end):
Essential geography, props, entrances/exits:
Continuity risks and information to withhold:
Visual question: what should the audience notice, feel or not know at each beat?
```

## Principle versus choice

It is broadly accepted craft practice to identify objectives, obstacles, changes and physical continuity before coverage. Whether the turn calls for a close-up, an unbroken wide, no cut at all, or a camera move is a directorial choice that must serve this scene’s point of view and tone—not a universal rule.
